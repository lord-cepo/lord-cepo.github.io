What does equipartition predict?

The molar heat capacity at constant volume is the derivative of molar internal energy with respect to temperature. For a classical gas, each of the three momentum components contributes kBT/2 per electron. Assuming one conduction electron per copper atom, we obtain

Uel=32RT,Cel=dUeldT=32R

A classical harmonic lattice adds 3R per mole of atoms because each direction of vibration has kinetic and potential energy. The fully classical model therefore gives 9R/2 ≈ 37.4 J/(mol·K).

What changes with Fermi–Dirac statistics?

When T ≪ TF, occupation changes occur mainly within a few kBT of EF. The fraction of states involved is of order T/TF. The low-temperature expansion gives

Cel≈π22RTTF,Cret≈12π45R(TΘD)3

To isolate the Pauli exclusion effect, both coloured curves use the same quantum Debye lattice. The blue curve adds the Fermi contribution and the orange curve adds the classical term 3R/2. We use TF ≈ 82,000 K from the free-electron model and ΘD = 345.8 K from low-temperature calorimetry. Experimental estimate of ΘD

What does the experiment measure?

Robie and colleagues heated a pure copper sample in an adiabatic calorimeter. The energy supplied, corrected for the apparatus contribution and divided by the temperature rise, gives the molar heat capacity. The points show seven measurements of Cp between 15 and 30 K. Over this range we use Cp ≈ CV for the comparison. Robie et al. (1976), Table 3

These points show that the classical model overestimates the heat capacity. Isolating the small linear electronic term requires lower-temperature measurements, such as those between 1 and 5 K. Corak et al. (1955)
