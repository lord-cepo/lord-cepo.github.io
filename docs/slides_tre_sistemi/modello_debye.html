From acoustic waves to the Debye heat capacity

Which frequencies contribute to heat capacity?

Grey shows the density of modes. Blue shows their contribution to heat capacity. Higher-frequency modes contribute more as temperature rises.

Debye, Einstein and the classical limit

Blue: Debye. Orange: Einstein, with a single frequency ωE = ωD chosen for comparison. Dashed grey: classical limit of 3R.

Three acoustic branches, many frequencies

In a three-dimensional solid, acoustic modes at small wave numbers have frequency ω ≃ vsq. In 3D the number of wave vectors with magnitude below q grows with the volume of a sphere, hence as q³. The resulting density of modes per frequency interval is proportional to ω².

Debye extends the linear dispersion to a cutoff frequency ωD. The cutoff is chosen so that counting all three polarizations yields the 3N modes of a solid with N atoms. The Debye temperature is ΘD = ℏωD/kB.

Each mode is a quantum oscillator

A mode has mean occupation 1/(ex − 1), where x = ℏω/(kBT). Differentiating its thermal energy with respect to T gives the mode’s heat capacity.

Cmodo=kBx2ex(ex−1)2

Integrate this contribution over all frequencies, weighted by the density of modes. Per mole of atoms, the result is

CV=9R(TΘD)3∫0ΘD/Tx4ex(ex−1)2dx

At low T, long-wavelength modes remain active. The integral approaches a constant and heat capacity falls as T³, giving the phonon term used in the copper example. The Einstein model gives an exponential decrease because every mode has the same finite frequency.
