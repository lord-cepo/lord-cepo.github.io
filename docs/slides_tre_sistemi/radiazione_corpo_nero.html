Heating a resistor

The resistor illustrates an ideal emitter. The colour uses the CIE visual response, with brightness compressed for screen display. The higher temperatures let you explore the model spectrum.

Spectrum as a function of wavelength

From photons to the Planck spectrum

Each radiation mode has energy hν per photon. At equilibrium, photons can be created and absorbed, so their chemical potential is zero. The Bose–Einstein distribution gives

⟨nν⟩=1ehν/(kBT)−1

As in the Debye model, count modes in three dimensions. The number per frequency interval grows as ν². Multiply by hν and the mean occupation, then change variables from frequency to wavelength to obtain the spectral radiance.

Bλ(T)=2hc2λ51ehc/(λkBT)−1

From visible radiation to perceived colour

Different wavelengths stimulate the eye differently. To reproduce the colour, integrate the visible spectrum against three functions describing this response and convert the result to RGB. At low temperatures, nearly all emission is infrared. As the temperature rises, red becomes visible first and shorter wavelengths increasingly contribute.

The plot divides each curve by its peak to show its shape even at low temperatures. The total power per unit area increases as T⁴. The marker shows the peak of Bλ, which shifts to shorter wavelengths as T increases.
